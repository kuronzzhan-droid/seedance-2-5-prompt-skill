# How to make an anthro short film (with "One Copper Coin" as the standard)

"One Copper Coin" is a 4 min 30 s short the author assembled from nine Seedance 2.5 segments: a German shepherd knight has one copper coin left in his purse and rent due tomorrow, so he heads out of town with his black panther partner to chase off a wild boar for silver. On every platform it has the highest views, like rate, and save rate of the author's films, and most of the views came from people outside the existing audience [author-tested, 2026-11]. "Grey Hill", made in the same period with more polished images, "The Brass Laurel", with a more complex plot, and "Broken String", with two lions, all fell far short. The difference is not prompt technique. It is the premise, the characters, and the pacing.

This skill turns what that film got right, and the director's notes from the time, into rules that can be followed. The specifics of prompt writing (timeline, asset roles, dialogue format) follow seedance-video-prompt and gpt-image-prompt; this file mentions them only where needed.

## 0. Three checks before starting

Start making images only when all three pass. If one fails, go back and change the concept; do not count on the visuals to rescue it later.

1. **A one-sentence predicament**: you can write one sentence of "archetype + concrete predicament + deadline or cost", and that sentence works as the title. Copper Coin is "a German shepherd knight with one copper coin in his purse and rent due tomorrow". If you cannot write it, the story needs backstory or lore to be understood.
2. **A contrasting pair**: the two leads are opposite in at least three of fur colour, silhouette, personality, and talkativeness. Copper Coin pairs a tan-and-cream, hot-blooded, chatty German shepherd with an all-black, purple-scarfed, quiet panther, and they can be told apart at phone-cover size. The two lions of "Broken String", close in both colour and silhouette, are the counter-example.
3. **A through-line prop**: one small thing that appears at the start, moves the story once in the middle, and pays off at the end. The copper coin is tipped out at the opening, tossed to choose a road in the middle, and at the end silver coins drop one by one beside the old copper. This is also what audiences remember and discuss most (the top-liked comment on "The Brass Laurel" was an interpretation of a wine cup). The prop gets its own reference image, and every segment writes the same colour and material.

## 1. Concept and script

### Story

- **Understandable with no backstory**: no world-building lecture, no history, no villain. A passer-by can click in with zero barrier.
- **Conflict comes from daily life, not from fighting**: money, work, weather, misunderstandings, looking after someone. The boar in Copper Coin turns out to be "just hungry" and is led away with apples. Fights are what video models do worst; when one is unavoidable, shoot only shadows, footsteps, and the result.
- **The simple one wins through kindness or instinct**: the lead can be guileless but not stupid. Viewers criticised "the shepherd is too dumb to believe", so make him the butt of a joke at most once per film and give him one win that comes from good-heartedness.
- **Every emotional turn has a visible cause**, and three to five seconds is enough. In "The Brass Laurel" the duke goes from "I won't go, too noisy" to sitting in the box of honour the next day with no cause shown, and viewers asked "love at first sight?".
- **End on one gentle action left unexplained**: the panther watches the sunset and quietly tips his own silver into the shepherd's purse.

### Segments

- One segment per 30 seconds, and one segment = **one place + one visible small event** (one joke or one flutter of the heart). Copper Coin's nine are: getting up and cooking, greetings on the main street, meeting on the bridge, the stolen sword and the coin toss at the fork, hitting a tree and the apples falling, being dragged off by the boar, landing in the mountain stream, luring the boar with apples and mending the fence, splitting the pay at sunset.
- **At least 5 places per film, with the characters always on their way to the next one.** Copper Coin has 10: bedroom, kitchen, main street, town gate, stone bridge, dirt road, fork, farm, spruce wood, mountain stream. "Grey Hill" keeps reusing the rain gallery, the hall, and the study, and feels closed in.
- **At least two body or everyday moments per film, the first within the opening 20 seconds**: sitting up in bed and stretching, pulling on a shirt on the way downstairs, working bare-chested, wringing out clothes after falling in the water, eating. One of the most-liked comments was about "the beauty getting out of bed".
- The first second is already a frame with action in which the lead's body or face is visible; establishing the setting comes after the event.

### Dialogue

- 4–6 lines per segment, none over 3 seconds, mostly reaction lines: 「遅い。」「悪い悪い！」「逆だ。」「……すまん。」. Not one line in the film explains the plot or voices a feeling.
- Whatever can be acted is not said. The pair's relationship rests entirely on actions: lifting the purse, stealing the sword, a hand reaching to pull the other out of the water, tipping in the silver.
- After the two meet, one line says what they are off to do today (director's note: "after they meet, say what today's job is").
- A joke needs a physical cause and a clear screen direction. "Charging off the wrong way", shot head-on the first time, was understood by no one; it worked only as a side-on wide composition with the town at frame left and the mountains at frame right.

### Acting

- Faces can play as big as animation: going cross-eyed at a water drop on the nose, puffed cheeks, an eye-roll, a raised brow. Every expression is a reaction to something, not a fixed "emotion signal". Do not use the same small action (a grin, a tail flick, a flattened ear) in every segment.
- When both are in frame, each does his own thing: one strides along swinging his arms, the other walks barefoot on the grass between the cart ruts.
- A stoic character needs small changes too: a raised brow, one corner of the mouth, narrowed eyes, an eye-roll. He shifts pose now and then and shows his build without seeming to: arms folded, sitting on the bridge rail spinning a dagger, stretching, one knee down with a hand on a post.

## 2. Character design: from sketch to 4K final

### 2.1 Design principles

- **A family look**: fur, leather, and sunlight are shot with real texture, while the faces act like animation; eyes are alive, with a highlight. Later films keep this look, and new projects use Wachhund's realistic full-body reference as the style reference.
- **One warm, one cool, each with one mark**: Wachhund has the grey-blue cloak and a crescent bracelet; Kern.V has the purple scarf, a crescent mark under one eye, and a silver ear ring. They are recognisable at cover size.
- **Write the setup first, then give each inner trait one visible design element.** The silhouette needs a shape, left and right can be deliberately asymmetric, and each character has one memorable point.
- **A face is designed**, not copied from a reference. If you only copy the reference face, the user will say "the face has no design".
- **All animal faces by default, no human faces** (unless a commission or the user specifies human characters). Leads use familiar species archetypes (German shepherd, black panther, wolf, dragon).
- **Every supporting character has his own design image, each different in species, height, age, and build**: Copper Coin's baker is a 2.05 m horse with a swimmer's build, and the farmer is a 1.75 m, forty-year-old ram with a wrestler's build. Give everyone the same six-pack body and the user will say "they just swapped heads". Passers-by are designed too: writing "sheep-headed townsfolk" yields real sheep, and "a young cat-headed anthro" yields a kitten.

### 2.2 The iteration process

1. Write the setup first, then find reference images (single figure, full body, adult, hand-drawn), write prompts, and give the user 2–3 **drawn** options to choose from; do not describe options in words only.
2. Run the ChatGPT web page and any second free channel at the same time, 2–4 images per prompt. Open all the chats together, and close each window once its image is saved.
3. Review with one reviewing model only, then zoom in yourself: first whether the face is an animal face, then hand and foot structure, left and right, and counts. Once the whole round is in, show the user the overview first and put your recommendation after it; users often pick the one that was scored low.
4. **Make small changes around the one the user likes**: when the user says "the feel of that one", rerun that image's original prompt and change only the point they named, leaving every other word untouched. Changing one trait adds one short sentence; phrases like "symmetrical" or "exactly the same" take over the whole body part.
5. **Tune one character at a time.** After two or three rounds of refining five characters together, the user's verdict was "they're all a bit off; I think each needs tuning on its own".
6. Spend money on 4K only once the look is settled: quote the price, get the user's yes, and produce 2–4 through the API for them to pick from.
7. Add the references video needs: clothed full body, bare-chested or base body, and a head close shot, all at eye level.

### 2.3 Example: how Wachhund was settled round by round

- **Profession and manner**: rounds 5 to 8 tried, in turn, a captain of the guard, a special-forces officer, a mercenary captain, a monster hunter, and a household knight; round 8's "silver temple knight" became the candidate. One watercolour of a white-and-gold eagle-man seen from behind served throughout as the only style reference.
- **Small changes around the candidate** (round 13, one change at a time): restoring the candidate → an alluring version → deep V-neck with long trousers (shorts "lacked presence") → shirt plus leg straps → lacing and a casual stance → taller (1.95 m) → the pose from the reference → the texture of a thick chest → fixing the hands.
- **Realistic 4K**: the chosen watercolour candidate, unscaled, as Image 1, changing only the art style to film-grade realistic CG; a clothed version and a base-body version. The user then confirmed these details one by one, and each confirmed item went into "confirmed appearance" to be copied into every later prompt:
  - Hands: "let's have gloves on both hands after all": a matching pair of half-finger gloves, showing five fingertips and short black claws.
  - Feet: settled from the base-body image. Broad, thick canine feet with the whole sole on the ground, a rounded thick instep, four short round toes, and a small dewclaw on the inner side. Narrow little dog paws are wrong.
  - Build: "portrait-grade muscle" is not enough. Write visible shapes: very thick pectorals with a deep cleft, broad shoulders and a narrow waist, a clear six-pack, lifted glutes, thick thighs, long legs. API prompts do not use the word "sexy".
  - Eyes: natural amber, not glowing. Words like "glowing", "translucent", or "crystal clear" make eyes glow; write an iris of deep golden brown inside and honey at the rim, fine radial texture, and one small moist highlight.
- **Lessons**: write down the user's words on the spot (time, exact words, what changed). Write the face only from the original candidate, not from later versions that have drifted, and leave the face alone when fixing something else ("the face has completely changed").

### 2.4 Writing notes (details in gpt-image-prompt)

- Hands: left unspecified, they come out human. By species, write the finger count, fur reaching the fingertips, and the colour and shape of the claws; the back or side of the hand faces the camera, holding something. Do not write paw pads facing the camera, which turns into a paw-print stamp.
- Fur: "satin", "velvet", and "smooth" make realistic fur look like bare skin; write visible strands and a fluffy edge.
- Edits: when following Image 1, write "as in Image 1" item by item, and for each change write "this one differs from Image 1: …". Change nothing the user did not mention.
- By default generated images are not fed back as references; the only exceptions are the specific cases the user has approved one by one. Video references always use the 4K finals.

## 3. Scenes

- One **empty scene image** per place, with no characters in it; where the characters stand is written in the prompt.
- Realistic photographic texture, rich colour, strong contrast. Default to clear daylight or golden sunset; overcast, rain, and night take no more than a third of the film, and those frames carry points of warm light.
- **Correct real-world sizes to the characters' height**: in Copper Coin's farm image the tree trunks were about 1 m thick and the apples about 40 cm, so the model shrank the crouching character and shortened his legs. Trunks half a metre thick and fist-sized apples fixed it.
- Scenes have living things in them: pigeons, sparks from the forge, someone watering flowers, running water, a flock of birds, townsfolk walking by.
- One light condition per segment. A change of sky is a change of segment.

## 4. Directing: shooting the story as 30-second segments

The first half was directed by the author. Below are the director's notes from the time, which later proved right.

### The director's shot sample (segment 1, getting up and leaving)

Facing the bed, he jolts upright, back rounded, abs slightly folded, face full of sleep → from the side, thighs and torso in frame, a quick big stretch that opens the chest and flank → down the stairs with a light step, trouser legs rolled, barefoot, bare-chested, pulling on a shirt as he goes → fried eggs and bacon, a profile close-up as he cooks, one ear flicking → he sits and eats happily, and as he picks up knife and fork he catches sight of a note with a crescent drawn on it by the table leg, his partner's voice sounding off-screen → a start, then he lifts the plate and wolfs down the rest → cut to boots on, sword in hand, out the door.

The structure to reuse: **body moment → everyday action → noticing something → reaction → out the door into the next place**.

### The director's notes after samples (they apply to every clip)

- The background must not be dead: add a "Scene motion:" section to every segment, saying how wind, water, flags, smoke, birds, and townsfolk are moving.
- More shots, and more interesting ones: push, pull, orbit, rotate, follow, rise and fall are all in play. One main event per shot, 2–5 seconds each, 8–10 shots for a segment with action and fewer for dialogue. Many shots each crammed with small actions lose the sense of motion, as "The Brass Laurel" did.
- When both are in frame, write what the two are doing at the same time.
- Every close and medium shot states the expression: brows, eyes (narrowed, wide, rolled, sidelong), mouth corners, nose and whiskers, plus ears and tail.
- Posture: Wachhund keeps craning his neck forward, so every segment writes "chest directly above the pelvis, neck rising straight up between the shoulders, chin level".
- The tail hangs naturally behind; write only the lower half swaying left and right, never "lashing widely".
- Continuity: a character who appears in the next shot is already in frame in the one before (as the shepherd runs out of the town gate, the panther is already sitting on the bridge rail). Check close shots for both ears.
- Replace a joke that is not working outright: "charging off the wrong way" became "he tosses the coin to choose a road, the coin rolls away down the dirt road, he chases it, and the panther says 「……合ってるな」".

### Fixed practice when writing prompts

- Images first, then write to the image: the scene section describes what the scene image actually shows, and then come positions, shots, and action.
- Each segment opens with "Opening frame:": where each character stands at second 0, which way he faces, both legs straight, and the height relation (the top of the panther's head at the shepherd's eye level).
- One main event per shot, closing with "Ends with: …", and the next shot picks up from there.
- Fix screen directions: "far off at frame left is the town they came from".
- Small props get a colour, shape, and count: "one old copper-red coin and one pale round wooden button".
- The state of clothing goes into every shot; written only in the character table, it gets ignored.
- For characters write only "which part moves how", with appearance as "as in Image 1". No similes, no inner thoughts, nothing off-screen, no negation words.
- Before a line, write the expression, the tone, and how wide the mouth opens. To hold a higher-pitched voice, write the line as a startled exclamation.
- Hard kanji words get misread (全財産 came out as ぜんざいさん, 銀貨 as 銀河): swap in words that read easily, and check by transcription after generation.
- List ambient sound by the second: the single "ting" of the coin landing in a palm, the longsword leaving its scabbard, boots skidding on the dirt road.

## 5. Production rhythm

1. Show the user the story beats and dialogue first, and generate once they agree. After Copper Coin's first round of 8 segments, 17 more clips were rerun; checking the dialogue beforehand would have saved more than half.
2. Submit all segments at 480P in one go, running in parallel, and look at the rhythm of the whole film first.
3. Review: one model scores each segment out of 35 and lists hard faults, every line is checked by transcription, and then the user sees it.
4. Fix only what is wrong. A problem in a single shot is fixed with a 4–5 second insert shot (only the characters and references it needs); only a change to the story reruns a whole segment.
5. Go to 720P after the user agrees. When resolution trades off against credits, give them the options and prices first and wait for their decision.

## 6. Edit and release

- **Second 0 is the film itself**: no title card, no chapter headings, no part cards, no name captions, no fade in from black. "Grey Hill" opened with a 10.5-second title card on black and was later replaced and re-uploaded on every platform.
- Score in 3–4 cues that follow the emotion, one track to one or two segments, with a downbeat landing on an action (in Copper Coin, the splash). Duck it about 11 dB under dialogue, with the whole mix around −16 LUFS.
- Subtitles are coloured by character, with the main language large and the spoken language small at the very bottom; subtitles need to be big enough.
- Title = archetype + predicament. Cover = the lead's face, large, + the through-line prop. Post once per platform, with no deleting and reposting.
- Four and a half minutes runs long for short-video apps: consider splitting it in two or cutting a separate 60-second highlight.

## 7. Check before delivering

- [ ] The one-sentence predicament works as a title and needs no backstory
- [ ] The lead pair contrast in three or more ways, each with one mark
- [ ] There is a through-line prop with its own reference image and a payoff at the end
- [ ] At least 5 places; mostly clear daylight or sunset
- [ ] A body or everyday moment within the first 20 seconds; second 0 is an action frame
- [ ] One place and one small event per segment; 4–6 short lines per segment
- [ ] Every emotional turn has a visible cause; fights show only shadows and results
- [ ] Every supporting character whose face appears has a design image, each with a different build
- [ ] Scene images have sizes corrected to the characters' height
- [ ] Every segment has "Opening frame:" and "Scene motion:", with one main event per shot and an "Ends with"
- [ ] The user has seen the story beats and dialogue
- [ ] No title card, chapter headings, or opening on black

## 8. What Copper Coin itself got wrong (avoid next time)

- The opening is still not fast enough: second 0 is an empty bed under a quilt, and the first 20 seconds are all indoors cooking, so the 3-second drop-off was clearly higher than usual. Next time the first shot is the sit-up and stretch, or a 3-second hook of "one copper coin tipped out of a purse" comes first.
- Viewers said the panther's "posture reads as a female body": a stoic, slender character still needs broad shoulders, a thick chest, and narrow hips.
- Small props multiply: one button came out as three. Write "exactly one" in prop shots, or drop minor props.
- The jokes thin out in the two middle segments in the wood and the stream; they could be compressed into one.
